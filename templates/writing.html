Between Ag Science and Industries, its greenhouses, and the Creamery, there’s a walk surrounded by young tulip trees that 50 years from now will be probably one of the most extraordinary places on campus. The trees might exceed the height of the buildings that surround them.

A common thing people say when they come back to campus after their careers is that the trees are taller than they remember them. They won’t stay medium-sized forever.

Last winter I found on the ground on a walk through a park a stick with very spiky wooden points at its ends. Occasionally, I would come across another one: on Pitt campus; around the other parks in my area; or when I went camping this past spring.

Eventually, this summer — after finding one of those branches on the ground with hard petals around it — I realized that they are what remains of tulip tree flowers after they’ve gone to fruit. They are apparently, alongside magnolias, among the phylum of the earliest flowering plants; their odd woody flowers justify that evolutionary position for me.

Tulip tree leaves were probably the first tree leaf I could definitively identify, I can recall talking in my childhood about how they look like some deer or critter took a bite out of them.

It turns out that leaf shape has a real biomechanical purpose — in high winds they roll up into cones to lessen the load on the long trunks of one of the tallest trees of our Northern Appalachian forests. When it’s windy, I venture up to the trees on that walk by the creamery and watch the cones develop as the wind gets stronger: research for my physics project on the subject.

The wind, though, is getting colder and stronger, and those leaves are losing their compliant nature. Already most of them instead of reconfiguring, to produce least drag have blown off of their trees.

Like the oaks, maples, cherries, dogwoods, sycamores, and cypresses through campus, tulip trees will be known only by their buds, bark, and branching patterns. The primary reds and yellows will again be the bricks of our buildings; the greens the grasses and hemlocks; and the blues, whites, pinks, and lilacs represented by our Happy Valley sky.

There is a certain dullness to a campus without leaves, but only of color — our trees have incredible character without them: in their statures, their habits, and their weird spiky flower remnants.

Get to know them: they won’t stay medium-sized forever.

One of the most interesting books I've read in the last few years is called "The Gardens of Emily Dickinson" which discusses the famous American poet's affinity for various plants and the ways she represented the inhabitants of her garden through her art. According to Judith Farr — the author of the book — Emily Dickinson had a fondness — among her other favorite flowers: the daylilies and daisies — for Monotropa uniflora: the 'ghost plant.'

The plant is not terribly common in the disturbed parks I called home for most of my life, so my first exposure to it was in that book. The second (as is becoming something of a pattern in the plants I describe on this blog) was on a hike up Mount Nittany. I recall being quite enthused upon spotting a patch of characteristic pale translucent 'candy canes' behind a perfectly ripe blueberry bush and feeling something like a New England poet among woodland inspirations must feel.

The ghost plant has no green at any stage in its life, sucking saccharide life-force from its tree peers through mycorrhizal fungi straws. From each stem out of the ground — monotropa — a single flower — uniflora — unfurls. They aren't the prettiest of ephemeral wildflowers in our forests; they don't compare to springbeauties, pinks, or trilliums in color or stature, and upon fertilization seem to almost burn and blacken. At the end of autumn all that's left is a blackened stalk propping up a tan seed head.

Its tiny seeds then disperse on the wind — to find another tree root to parasitize.

There is, of course, a different perspective on its ghostly stature.

There's something to be said for the non-ecological niche of the ghost plant in American forests. It is not a parasite in the mind of Emily Dickinson, but a graceful, melancholy member of Northeast woodlands. Like a thick Monday morning fog or the first glistening frost on the HUB lawn, it's not trying to be a cheerful daffodil or tulip, but it's also not therefore less representative of the beauty of life.

In fact, in a letter to her friend addressing the friend's recent gift of a drawing of a bunch of the flowers, she terms them "the preferred flower of life."

This time last year my botany professor brought to class a tangled ball of spurge.

"Can anyone tell me what family this is in?"

She broke one of the many viny yarn-width stems and walked around the classroom to show us the milky latex sap oozing from the wound.

"I often see a species of this family in peoples' windows around christmastime… We talked about it last week. Anyone?"

Poinsettias are the last flora you'd expect to be associated with such a weedy ground-dweller; with jealousy its leaves turn red to mimic the petals of the prettier-flowered plants in winter greenhouses.

Spurge is abundant in Pittsburgh; it grows between sidewalk cracks, on ice-damaged streets, on the sides of buildings: anywhere it's given the chance. In that grayer place, one grows to appreciate those aspects of nature unfurling where they shouldn't.

Outside the building after class I spotted a clump of the plant between two sections of sidewalk, feeding off the concrete. Sure enough, when I plucked a leaf, a milky latex sap oozed: Euphorbiaceae family.

To this day, every time I see one on the street, I think of that lecture. I think of the poinsettia, embarrassed of its city-dweller kin. I think of their strange little flat flowers which make the family easy to identify.

I didn't realize how few spurges I've seen recently until I saw one downtown a few days ago.

Penn State's greenery was one of the factors which made me decide to come here for college. On my first tour I was impressed by the number of native Pennsylvania plants in the gardens: how few silly yellow daffodils there are. I remain impressed by our choices of botanical features: the tulip trees by the Ag Science and Industries building, the pachysandra by the gazebo at the edge of the HUB lawn, and the azalea by the entrance to the west Atherton courtyard.

I do miss the spurges though. For their unique flowers, but also for their nature to paint outside the lines.

In our efforts to domesticate nature on our campus – in pulling and spraying the weeds – we also lose its inherent creativity.

The poinsettia fits inside the shiny plastic wrapping we place around it in society. The spurge paints wherever it wants. Their flowers are the same; which will define the family?

A balmy late summer sunset breeze wafts through glimmering, flaming plumes of goldenrod lining the meandering road to the big green mountain. Pedalling quicker now, I take a breath of sweet, floral air.

And another.

And another.

Before long, I'm at that 'T' in the road where cornfields turn to forests. What used to be calm air is now a jarring stop-start of exhaust as cars drive past my stop sign at what feels like fifty miles an hour. I find an opening and immediately jump into a sprint; suddenly within those winds.

I believe in biking through the country.

Growing up a pedestrian in the city, I learned from a young age that pickup trucks don't stop if you're in the street. On long drives through the Laurel Highlands to get to camping sites with friends, we passed by Gadsden flags, homemade "TRUMP 2020" billboards, and big loud pickup trucks bedazzled with both. When I learned to drive I understood that pickup trucks shouldn't be expected to execute a zipper merge. I nodded my agreement when my Orgo professor cursed a pickup truck which had — in running a red light — ruined his prized car from college, broken a number of his bones, and cancelled many of our lectures.

In all these experiences, I associated more and more the unyielding pickup truck with the image of the unyielding person I thought drove it — the people flying confederate flags on their lawns — the kids who called my friends slurs — the people on the internet posting "your body my choice" — the politicians who would rather see innocent civilians killed abroad with our advanced weapons than citizens given homes with our apparently enormous financial prowess.

Where did their humanity go?

Splotches of sunlight flit across my face from between leaves of the canopy as I ratchet up my gears to keep up with the car ahead of me. I whisper under my hard breathing the name of the street I need to remember to turn left on. Mountain Road. Mountain Road. Mountain Road. But there are only trees. I glance behind me and shit there's a pickup truck. There's a margin, but it's littered with sticks and rocks, so I can only signal my intention to stay to the right.

The truck zooms closer and closer, and I think of the inevitable: me, lying on a hospital bed, my mom standing by my side. "This is why we didn't let you bike to school!" she would say. I had only wanted to escape my studies for the woods; what a dreadful way for a beautiful day to go.

All of a sudden, a glance to my left reveals a pickup truck, fully in the opposing lane, passing with more space than I had ever myself passed a biker in Pittsburgh — three whole city bike lane lengths between us.

I waved a wave of enormous thanks, and of disbelief, to that "Trump 2024"-sticker-clad pickup truck, and through that flat back window, the driver returned a wave. As I signalled and turned onto Mountain Road, I had a massive smile on my face: so some humanity remains after all.

And so I watched from between trees on the peak of the big green mountain that evening as the wood lilies, beebalms, and violets of a late summer sunset fell over our forests, our corn fields, our animal pastures, our industrial factories, our campus, our sprawling suburbia, and State College, and I contemplated that yielding pickup truck.

I believe in biking on country roads.

The temperature of the colors of nature tends to intensify through the fall; the leaves go from yellow to red, goldenrods burst into bright flame, and the remaining greens eventually dull from exposure.

A few individuals seem to delight in their nonconformity to this rule; the aster is one of them, coming in shades of light purple and blue from September through the end of November.

In my neighborhood after the long walk through the park back from high school I was often surprised by the abundance of wild flora on little patches of unmaintained grass and among hedges. Through a surprising duration of the fall, the aster is the dominant plant in those areas; at points along the sidewalks of my neighborhood, one might find their little bright flowers poking out of hedges on one side and unfurling from the gap between the sidewalk and the soil on the other.

In State College (as mentioned in my last post) asters are often too a personality of the journey rather than the destination; they occur infrequently on campus, but can be spotted nearly anywhere else, from the sides of the road downtown to the edges of our Centre County cornfields. The aster's apparent locational preferences are not, however, of its sprawling, hardy nature – they inhabit rhododendron thickets just as abundantly as their more delicate peers – but a symptom of the human perception of many plainer flowers as "weeds."

I certainly would protest that designation. Our native flora shouldn't be dismissed.

Botany is maybe the clearest colonial science of them all – its western roots lie in the "discovery" and harvest of the most colorful plants overseas to beautify European gardens. The plants we choose to grow in our autumn gardens are usually the most vibrant symbols of this plunder: the canna lily, stonecrop, garden mum, etc. To grow those flowers, we pull the bright asters as weeds. Through the season, the gardens we plucked from overseas grow unfamiliar with our temperate weather and grow duller even than the roadsides and in-between spaces still inhabited by the wild asters.

At the end of autumn in central Pennsylvania, when the browned leaves have long been blown off city streets and pedestrian paths, you'll find the wild asters are still blooming, having persisted through the heat of autumn in their scandalous blues, whites, and purples, in every place we've overlooked.

Avenging their kin, the cold-colored asters conspire to bring winter.
